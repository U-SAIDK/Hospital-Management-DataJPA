# Cascade & orphanRemoval

## 1. Definition

**Cascade** tells JPA to propagate an operation performed on a parent entity to its associated
child entities, so you don't have to call the same operation on each child yourself. It is
configured per association via `cascade = {CascadeType.X, ...}`. **`orphanRemoval = true`** is a
stronger, related setting: it deletes a child automatically when that child is **removed from its
parent's ownership** — either by nulling/clearing the reference or by deleting the parent — treating
the child as something that cannot exist on its own.

The `CascadeType` options are: `PERSIST`, `MERGE`, `REMOVE`, `REFRESH`, `DETACH`, and `ALL` (all of
the previous).

## 2. Core Idea

Cascade answers "when I `persist`/`remove`/`merge` the parent, what happens to the children?"
`orphanRemoval` answers "when a child is disconnected from its parent, should it be deleted?" They
overlap on parent-deletion (both can delete children when the parent is removed) but differ on
*dereferencing*: only `orphanRemoval` deletes a child that is simply unlinked from a still-alive
parent. This project uses both, with two contrasting configurations that show exactly why you'd pick
one over the other.

## 3. Why We Need It

- Without cascade, saving an aggregate means manually saving each part in the right order, and
  deleting means manually deleting children first to respect FK constraints — error-prone
  boilerplate.
- Without `orphanRemoval`, "unassigning" a wholly-owned child (like a patient's insurance) would
  leave an orphan row behind, or force an explicit `delete()` call.
- Getting the cascade *scope* wrong is dangerous: too broad (e.g. `REMOVE`/`ALL` on a shared
  association) can delete rows other entities still need. Choosing the minimal cascade that models
  the real ownership is the goal.

## 4. Internal Working

- **Cascade at flush.** When Hibernate flushes a parent operation, it walks each cascaded association
  and applies the same operation to the children. `PERSIST` on a parent INSERT also INSERTs new
  children; `REMOVE` on a parent DELETE also DELETEs children; `MERGE` propagates a merge, etc.
- **orphanRemoval detection.** For an `orphanRemoval=true` association, Hibernate tracks the child
  reference. If, at flush, a previously-referenced child is no longer reachable through the parent
  (reference nulled, or removed from the collection), Hibernate schedules a DELETE for that now-orphan
  child — independently of any `CascadeType.REMOVE`.
- **Ownership requirement.** Both features act through the *managed parent* and the association
  mapping. The FK-owning side and the persistence context's dirty checking are what surface the
  change; the child rows are written/deleted at the parent's flush.

Key distinction: `CascadeType.REMOVE` fires only when the **parent itself is removed**;
`orphanRemoval` *also* fires when the child is merely **dereferenced** from a surviving parent.

## 5. Flow Diagram (ASCII)

```
 CascadeType.ALL + orphanRemoval  (Patient.insurance)
 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   patient.setInsurance(newIns)   ──► flush ──► INSERT insurance   (PERSIST cascades)
   patient.setInsurance(null)     ──► flush ──► DELETE insurance   (orphanRemoval fires)
   delete(patient)                ──► flush ──► DELETE insurance,   (REMOVE cascades)
                                                then DELETE patient

 CascadeType.REMOVE + orphanRemoval  (Patient.appointments)
 ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   delete(patient)   ──► flush ──► DELETE each appointment, then DELETE patient  (REMOVE cascades)
   (NOTE: no PERSIST cascade here — a new Appointment must be saved via its own repository,
    which is exactly what AppointmentService.createNewAppointment does)
```

## 6. Real-world Analogy

Think of a patient's **insurance policy** vs their **appointments**.

- The insurance policy is *part of* the patient's file — it has no meaning without them. Attach a new
  policy and it's filed with the patient (PERSIST cascade); tear the policy out of the file and it's
  shredded (orphanRemoval); close the whole file and the policy goes with it (REMOVE cascade). That's
  `cascade = ALL, orphanRemoval = true`.
- Appointments are *scheduled entries* that a receptionist creates through the appointment book
  (their own repository), not by stuffing them into the patient file. But if the patient's whole file
  is closed, their appointments are cancelled with it. That's `cascade = REMOVE, orphanRemoval = true`
  — deletion follows the patient, but creation does not.

## 7. How THIS PROJECT Uses It

Two contrasting mappings on `entity/Patient.java`:

- **`Patient.insurance` — `@OneToOne(cascade = {CascadeType.ALL}, orphanRemoval = true)` (owning
  side, `@JoinColumn(name = "patient_insurance_id")`).** The patient *fully owns* the insurance's
  lifecycle. `ALL` means saving the patient can save a new insurance, and deleting the patient deletes
  the insurance; `orphanRemoval` means simply nulling `patient.insurance` deletes the insurance row.
  This is appropriate because an `Insurance` in this model exists only as a patient's insurance — the
  inverse side `Insurance.patient` is `@OneToOne(mappedBy = "insurance")` with no FK of its own.
- **`Patient.appointments` — `@OneToMany(mappedBy = "patient", cascade = {CascadeType.REMOVE},
  orphanRemoval = true, fetch = FetchType.EAGER)`.** Only `REMOVE` cascades, **not** `ALL`. Deleting a
  patient deletes their appointments (and removing one from the collection deletes that appointment),
  but there is deliberately **no `PERSIST` cascade** — appointments are created through their own
  service/repository (`AppointmentService.createNewAppointment` calls
  `appointmentRepository.save(appointment)` directly), not by cascading from a patient save. This
  models appointments as independently-created but patient-lifecycle-dependent.

**Why `ALL` for insurance but only `REMOVE` for appointments?** Insurance is a private, wholly-owned
part of the patient aggregate, so the patient should drive *every* operation (persist/merge/remove) —
`ALL` is correct. Appointments are shared with `Doctor` and are created via a booking flow, so
cascading `PERSIST`/`MERGE` from `Patient` would be wrong (you'd risk creating/altering appointments
as a side effect of saving a patient); the only lifecycle event that genuinely *should* follow the
patient is deletion — hence `REMOVE` only.

## 8. Code Example From This Project

The mappings:

```java
// entity/Patient.java
@OneToOne(cascade = {CascadeType.ALL}, orphanRemoval = true)
@JoinColumn(name = "patient_insurance_id") // owning side
private Insurance insurance;

@OneToMany(mappedBy = "patient", cascade = {CascadeType.REMOVE}, orphanRemoval = true, fetch = FetchType.EAGER)
private List<Appointment> appointments = new ArrayList<>();
```

The orphanRemoval trigger in action:

```java
// service/InsuranceService.java
@Transactional
public Patient disaccociateInsuranceFromPatient(Long patientId) {
    Patient patient = patientRepository.findById(patientId)
            .orElseThrow(() -> new EntityNotFoundException("Patient not found with id: " + patientId));

    // Patient.insurance is declared with orphanRemoval=true, so nulling the reference on a managed
    // entity doesn't just clear the FK - Hibernate treats the Insurance as "orphaned" and issues a
    // DELETE for it at flush time. Without orphanRemoval this would only update the FK to null.
    patient.setInsurance(null);
    return patient;
}
```

And the cascade=ALL persist path:

```java
// service/InsuranceService.java
@Transactional
public Patient assignInsuranceToPatient(Insurance insurance, Long patientId) {
    Patient patient = patientRepository.findById(patientId)
            .orElseThrow(() -> new EntityNotFoundException("Patient not found with id: " + patientId));

    // cascade=ALL cascades the new Insurance's own insert; dirty checking on the managed `patient`
    // is what actually persists the FK. The line below is purely in-memory bookkeeping.
    patient.setInsurance(insurance);
    insurance.setPatient(patient); // bidirectional consistency maintainence

    return patient;
}
```

## 9. Line-by-Line Explanation

Walking `disaccociateInsuranceFromPatient` end to end:

- `@Transactional` — opens the context and keeps `patient` managed so the coming change is tracked
  and flushed; without it the orphan wouldn't be detected.
- `patientRepository.findById(patientId)` — loads a **managed** `Patient`; its currently-referenced
  `Insurance` is now known to the context as the child of this association.
- `patient.setInsurance(null)` — dereferences the child. Because the association is
  `orphanRemoval=true`, at flush Hibernate sees the previously-referenced `Insurance` is no longer
  reachable through the patient and schedules a **DELETE of the Insurance row** — not merely a
  `UPDATE patient SET patient_insurance_id = null`. No `insuranceRepository.delete(...)` is written.
- `return patient` — the method commits; the flush runs, the DELETE executes. (Contrast: without
  `orphanRemoval`, the same code would only null the FK and leave an orphaned insurance row behind.)

For `assignInsuranceToPatient`:

- `patient.setInsurance(insurance)` — links a (possibly new/transient) `Insurance` to the managed
  patient. `CascadeType.ALL` includes `PERSIST`, so if the insurance is new it gets INSERTed as part
  of the patient's flush; the owning-side FK is set by dirty checking on `patient`.
- `insurance.setPatient(patient)` — inverse-side, in-memory only (the FK lives on `Patient`), kept
  consistent for correct in-context navigation.

## 10. Common Mistakes

- **Confusing `CascadeType.REMOVE` with `orphanRemoval`.** `REMOVE` deletes children only when the
  *parent* is deleted; only `orphanRemoval` deletes a child that is *dereferenced* from a surviving
  parent — the exact behavior `disaccociateInsuranceFromPatient` relies on.
- **Using `CascadeType.ALL` on a shared association.** If appointments used `ALL`, saving a patient
  could persist/merge appointments as a side effect, and they're shared with `Doctor` — hence the
  intentional `REMOVE`-only choice.
- **Expecting nulling a FK to delete the row without `orphanRemoval`.** It would just null the FK.
- **Nulling from the inverse side.** Setting `insurance.setPatient(null)` (the `mappedBy` side) does
  nothing to the DB — only the owning `Patient.insurance` controls the FK and triggers orphanRemoval.
- **Assuming appointments can be created by cascade from Patient.** There's no `PERSIST` cascade on
  `Patient.appointments`; new appointments must be `save()`d via `AppointmentService`.

## 11. Best Practices

- Choose the **narrowest** cascade that models real ownership: `ALL` for a wholly-owned private child
  (`Patient.insurance`), a single type like `REMOVE` when only that lifecycle event should propagate
  (`Patient.appointments`).
- Use `orphanRemoval = true` only for genuine parent-owned children whose existence is meaningless
  without the parent — insurance qualifies; a shared entity would not.
- Perform cascade/orphanRemoval-driven changes on the **managed owning side** inside a transaction,
  as both `InsuranceService` methods do.
- Don't cascade `PERSIST`/`MERGE` into entities that have their own creation flow (appointments) —
  keep their lifecycle in their own service.

## 12. Performance Notes

- `orphanRemoval` and `REMOVE` cascades issue a DELETE **per child row** at flush (not a single bulk
  delete), so deleting a patient with many appointments emits many DELETEs — fine at this scale, worth
  knowing for large collections.
- `Patient.appointments` is also `fetch = EAGER`, so a delete-cascade first needs the collection
  loaded; the EAGER load and per-row deletes compound on large sets (a reason production code often
  keeps such collections LAZY — see `NOTES/11. Lazy vs Eager Loading.md`).
- Cascaded persists batch into the parent's flush, saving separate round trips versus manually saving
  each child.

## 13. Interview Questions

- **Q: Difference between `CascadeType.REMOVE` and `orphanRemoval = true`?**
  A: `REMOVE` deletes children when the parent is deleted; `orphanRemoval` additionally deletes a
  child when it is dereferenced from a surviving parent (e.g. `patient.setInsurance(null)`).
- **Q: Why is `Patient.insurance` `cascade = ALL` but `Patient.appointments` only `REMOVE`?**
  A: Insurance is a private, wholly-owned child so the patient should drive every operation;
  appointments are shared with `Doctor` and created via their own service, so only deletion should
  cascade from the patient.
- **Q: In `disaccociateInsuranceFromPatient`, why does setting the field to null delete a row?**
  A: `Patient.insurance` has `orphanRemoval = true`; nulling the reference on the managed patient
  makes the insurance an orphan, and Hibernate DELETEs it at flush.
- **Q: Would nulling `Insurance.patient` (the inverse side) delete anything?**
  A: No — the FK and orphanRemoval live on the owning `Patient.insurance` side; the `mappedBy` side
  has no DB effect.

## 14. Summary

Cascade propagates parent operations to children; `orphanRemoval` deletes a child that is unlinked
from its parent. This project contrasts the two ownership models on `Patient`:
`insurance` is `cascade = ALL, orphanRemoval = true` (a fully-owned private child — persist, merge,
and delete all follow the patient, and unassigning deletes it), while `appointments` is
`cascade = REMOVE, orphanRemoval = true` (deletion follows the patient, but creation goes through
`AppointmentService`, never a cascade). `InsuranceService.disaccociateInsuranceFromPatient` is the
end-to-end orphanRemoval demonstration: null the managed patient's `insurance` reference and
Hibernate issues a DELETE at flush, no explicit repository call required.
